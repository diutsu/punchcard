# Catalogue of LLM tells

Source: Wikipedia, "Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing. Fetched 2026-11-14 as raw wikitext (179,955 bytes, 1,452 lines, read in full). Paraphrased, not quoted. The page targets Wikipedia articles, drafts and comments, so tells that only make sense there (edit summaries, AfC drafts, wikitext templates, user pages) are translated to their general form or left out.

## How to use this catalogue

- The signs are observations, not rules. Human writers produce every one of them, and models trained on human text copy them. A cluster of tells counts for more than one hit.
- A tell points at a deeper problem: unsupported claims, invented sources, generic filler where a specific fact should be. Removing the surface tell does not repair the problem, and a polished sentence that stays unsupported makes detection harder. Fix the content, then the wording.
- Detection tools and human judgment are both unreliable. Studies cited on the page put untrained readers near chance, and detector tools make non-trivial errors. Use this list to find weak sentences in your own draft, not to accuse anyone.
- Model habits shift between versions. Words and patterns that dominated 2023 output faded by 2025, and newer models suppress some old tells while adding others. Expect this file to age.
- Why the tells exist: a model drifts toward the statistically likely phrasing, so specific, odd or nuanced facts get replaced by generic, upbeat statements. The subject gets less specific and more inflated at the same time. Specific facts are the cure.

Caveat that governs every pass: after the prose edit, verify the content. Open each link, check each quote against its source, re-run each command and compare the output.

## Content

### Significance, legacy and broader trends

Looks like: a minor fact gets tied to a "broader movement", "pivotal moment", "lasting legacy" or "shift". Debates and public discussion appear around a subject that has none. Ordinary topics such as a place name or a population figure get a closing remark about importance. Sometimes a hedge admits the topic is minor, then claims its importance anyway. Species and places get inflated ties to the wider ecosystem, region or culture, and conservation status is belabored even when it is unknown.

Example: "The library opened in 1998, marking a pivotal moment in the city's cultural evolution."

Write instead: the fact, plus a measurable effect if one exists ("The library opened in 1998 and lent 40,000 books in its first year"). Delete the importance claim.

Phrases to watch: stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a crucial/pivotal/vital/key role or moment, underscores/highlights its importance, reflects broader, symbolizing its enduring, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, generated debate, prompted broader reflection.

Newer variant (2026): a hedge that says a figure or detail should not be taken as exact, followed by editorializing about why the distinction matters. State the uncertainty once, with its cause.

### Emphasis on notability, sources and media coverage

Looks like: the text proves the subject matters by listing where it was covered ("featured in", "profiled by", "documented in regional press"), and names the kind of outlet (trade, local, national). The wording often echoes notability rules from the platform's guidelines. The cited outlets frequently do not say the attributed thing, and sometimes the reference does not exist. Seen more in 2025 and later output.

This differs from a human press release: the focus here is the sources themselves, their type, and their availability, rather than what they report.

Write instead: state what a named source reported, with a link. If no specific report exists, drop the sentence.

Phrases to watch: independent coverage, local/regional/national media outlets, trade publications, cited/featured/profiled in.

### Emphasis on identification by primary or tertiary sources

Looks like: "His website identifies him as...", "his profile lists...", "records show that...". The existence of a database, filing or personal page is stressed instead of the fact. Common from 2026.

Write instead: state the fact and cite the source in a reference, without narrating the source.

### Emphasis on online presence

Looks like: "maintains an active social media presence", "strong digital footprint". Rare in human writing before about 2024.

Write instead: delete, or give the account and a figure if the presence is relevant.

### Superficial analysis

Looks like: a sentence ends with a present-participle clause that comments on significance, impact or recognition ("...highlighting its relevance", "...ensuring continued growth", "...reflecting the region's culture"). Often attached to vague third parties, and with search-enabled tools, to a named source that never said it. Often part of a three-item run that makes the analysis feel full.

Write instead: cut the clause, or split it into its own sentence with evidence and a named source. Attribute opinions to the person who held them.

Phrases to watch: highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating, fostering, encompassing, enhancing, valuable insights, aligns with, resonates with.

### Vague expression of connection or association

Looks like: "connected to", "in association with", "associated with" where a direct relation exists ("was CEO of", "taught at"). Often combined with promotional buzzwords, sometimes stacked in one paragraph. Common in 2026 output.

Write instead: the actual relation: owns, founded, calls, replaces, causes, taught.

### Vague attribution and overgeneralized opinion

Looks like: "experts argue", "industry reports", "observers have cited", "some critics say". Views from one or two sources are presented as widely held. Several "reviewers" are mentioned while one is cited. A list of examples is implied to be non-exhaustive ("such as...") with no sign that others exist.

Write instead: name the person or publication and link it, or delete the claim. Say "one reviewer" when there is one.

Phrases to watch: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, several sources (when few are cited), such as before a full list.

### Outline-like "challenges and future prospects" passages

Looks like: a section or closing paragraph that opens "Despite its strengths, X faces challenges", lists a few generic ones, then ends on a vague positive note or speculation about future initiatives. Appears at the end of rigidly outlined pieces, sometimes with a separate "Future outlook" section. The tell is the fixed formula, not the mention of difficulties.

Write instead: document real, dated problems with sources, or omit the section. End on the last fact.

Phrases to watch: Despite its... faces several challenges, Despite these challenges, Challenges and legacy, Future outlook, future directions.

### "Awards and recognition" section

Looks like: headings in the form "X and Y", above all "Awards and recognition" or a bare "Recognition" section, even for subjects with no awards. Much more common in machine text than in human writing, even among promotional human articles.

Write instead: list only awards that exist, each with issuer and year, under a plain heading.

## Language and grammar

Machine text shows consistent syntax and word-choice patterns regardless of topic, which gives it a recognizable voice. Some models drift further from human writing than others.

### Density of "AI vocabulary"

Studies show some words rose sharply in text after late 2022 and tend to travel together. One or two in a passage can be chance. Many in one passage, in text written after 2022, is one of the strongest tells. A word being overused does not make its synonyms suspect. Check context: "underscore" can mean a literal underline character or incidental music.

Word list (flag as a cluster, not alone): additionally (sentence-initial), align with, boasts (for "has"), bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, intricacies, key, landscape (abstract), meticulous, meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry (abstract), testament, underscore (verb), valuable, vibrant.

By period, roughly:

- 2023 to mid-2024: additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate, interplay, key, landscape, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant.
- Mid-2024 to mid-2025: align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant.
- Mid-2025 to mid-2026: emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus the source-coverage phrases above.
- Mid-2026 onward: challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally. Output of this era leans on commas and periods with short sentences, roughly 8 to 20 words.
- Grok output leans on pseudo-scientific words: causal, empirical, correlate, and keeps using underscore.

Write instead: the plain word ("use", "show", "important", "has"), or cut the sentence if it carries only the flourish.

### Avoiding "is" and "are"

Looks like: "serves as", "stands as", "marks", "represents", "functions as", "boasts", "features", "offers", "maintains", "refers to" in place of "is" or "has". Recent output adds longer versions such as "began his career as" or "ventured into politics as" instead of "was". Studies found "is/are" dropped by more than 10 percent in academic writing in 2023, and models asked to revise text remove them. In lead sentences, "X refers to..." treats the subject as a word instead of defining the thing.

Do not confuse this with "has" in the perfect tense ("has been featured").

Write instead: "is" and "has". Define the thing directly.

Phrases to watch: serves as, stands as, marks, functions as, operates as, represents a, boasts a, features, offers a, maintains, refers to.

### Negative parallelism

Looks like: the text corrects a misconception no one held, or reframes the subject by denying one trait and asserting another. Humans do this in myth-busting pieces, but it is a stereotypical machine habit.

Forms:

- Not just X, but also Y: "not only X but Y", "it is not just X, it is Y", "doesn't just X; it Y".
- Not X, but Y: "it's not X, it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", "not a mirror but a portal". Can span two sentences.
- Y rather than X (reversed, common in newer output): "Y rather than X", "acts as a bridge rather than a replacement", "should be treated as symbolic rather than literal".

Example: "This is not a delay, it is a design choice."

Write instead: state Y. Mention X only when a reader plausibly believes it. "Not only... but also" is fine when both halves are true and the reader might expect only one.

### Defining list-like or broad titles as proper nouns

Looks like: a list or generic-topic article opens by naming its own title as if it were an entity ("The List of songs about Mexico is a curated compilation of...", "X refers to..."). The opening sentence should state what the subject is, not describe the page.

Write instead: open with the subject in natural words ("Mexico has inspired songs in every genre; the notable ones are listed below").

### Rule of three

Looks like: three adjectives, three nouns or three short phrases by habit ("tiles, metals and plastics"), often to make shallow analysis look complete. The sign is stronger in places where nobody bothers with flourish, such as short messages or change notes.

Write instead: list the items that exist, which may be two or five.

## Style

- Title case in headings. Use sentence case.
- Bold scattered through prose, or asterisks used as a poor imitation of bold. Bold a defined term at most.
- Vertical lists where each item starts with a bold label and a colon (inline-header lists). Write prose or a plain list.
- Wrong heading levels: a level 1 heading repeating the page title, or several level 1 headings in one piece.
- Emoji at the start of headings or list items.
- Curly quotation marks and apostrophes in text that otherwise uses straight ones (a weak sign alone).
- Overused em dashes, formulaic and "punched up", often with spaces around them, mostly in discussion text. Use commas, colons or periods. This tell is weak alone and fading: some recent models suppress em dashes entirely and then lean on commas and periods with little punctuation variety, which is its own pattern. Judge a text by its range of punctuation and rhythm, not by dash count.
- Little repetition of words: older models swap a term for synonyms at every recurrence ("the constraints", then "the confines", then "the norms"). Use one term for one referent. Non-native writers may avoid repeats on purpose, and separately generated pieces may not show this.

## Communication intended for the user

Chatbot correspondence pasted into the document, often in a note or comment, because the author forgot to remove it.

### Collaborative and conversational text

Looks like: offers and openers ("I hope this helps", "Certainly!", "Of course!", "You're absolutely right!", "Would you like...", "let me know", "is there anything else"), instructions to the author ("check sources before publishing", "delete this section before submission", "here is a template you can copy"), notes about the draft's own process, and text that cuts off mid-sentence because the chat reply ran out. Sometimes hidden in comments or placeholder blocks.

Write instead: delete. A document speaks to its reader, not to the person who prompted it.

Phrases to watch: I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, You're absolutely right!, Would you like, is there anything else, let me know, more detailed breakdown, here is a.

### Hedging disclaimers about source availability and usage

Looks like: the text points at its own gaps ("details are limited", "not widely documented", "based on available information", "in the provided sources") and then fills the gap with speculation about what is "likely". For a person, it claims they "maintain a low profile". The claim of absence is itself unverified guesswork.

Three eras:

- 2023-24: didactic cautions ("it is important to note", "it is crucial to differentiate", "always check before you use"), advice to an imagined reader, and disambiguation notes.
- 2024-25: search-enabled tools that failed to find a source say the information is unavailable and guess at it.
- Mid-2026 onward: corrective framings. They tell the reader to treat a source or claim as one thing "rather than" another, say the information "does not by itself establish" a firm conclusion, or say a topic "should not be confused" with another (the last is a weaker sign). Rare before mid-2026.

Write instead: state what you checked and what you found ("not found in the 2024 report"), or state the fact. Do not narrate what the sources lack.

### Placeholders and fill-in templates

Looks like: bracketed blanks left unfilled ("[Your name]", "[insert date]", "[Describe the section]", "[link to source]"), date stubs ("2025-XX-XX", "2022-11-XX") including in access or publication dates, URL stubs ("INSERT_SOURCE_URL_30", "PASTE_..._HERE"), and example.com links with numbered subpages ("example.com/source1"). A few legitimate templates ship comments or stubs, so check whether a template, not a chatbot, wrote it.

Write instead: fill each placeholder from a real source or delete the line.

## Markup irregularities

- Markdown in a field that renders something else: asterisks for bold, number signs for headings, `---` as dividers, link syntax in the wrong order, whole answers wrapped in triple backticks with a language tag.
- Leaked tool or citation markers: strings like `contentReference[oaicite:1]`, `turn0search`, `oaicite`. Unambiguous. Each chatbot leaves its own pattern.
- Invented structure: categories that do not exist, templates that do not exist, maintenance banners that no one placed (including a "declined" or "reviewed" banner on a new draft).
- Footnote return arrows (a left-hooked arrow after each footnote) copied from a chat interface.
- Bizarre stray HTML or wrong-place formatting marks are more often an editor bug or a conversion tool bug than a model.

## Citations

A stylish citation that cannot be checked is worse than none.

- Broken links: several dead or never-archived links in a new piece strongly suggest invented sources. A single dead link is normal link rot. Rule out paywalls, library-only access, and links mangled by scripts before concluding anything.
- Invalid identifiers: an ISBN with a failing checksum, a DOI that does not resolve.
- DOIs that resolve to an unrelated paper: authors, title and journal look real but the DOI belongs to something else. Check that the author could have written it (dates) and that the issue contains it. Low-numbered database IDs that lead to unrelated papers can also be a tool bug, not a model.
- Book citations without page numbers, or with page numbers that do not support the claim, on a broad topic, with no link to a readable copy. Search the book for the claim.
- Unconventional reference mechanics: reuse syntax that does not work, a reference attached after every sentence, references defined in a list but never cited in the body (or cited but undefined; copying from another page also does this).
- Tracking parameters: `utm_source=openai`, `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `utm_source=copilot.com`, `referrer=grok.com` in URLs. This shows a chatbot was involved, not necessarily that it wrote the prose; some people use chatbots only to find citations.
- Outdated access dates on citations, or copy-pasted ones, in a piece written much later. Weak sign: newer tools rarely do it, and legitimate cases exist.

Write instead: open every source, confirm it says what the sentence says, and cite the primary record without tracking parameters.

## Comment-specific indicators (discussion and review text)

Beyond the tells above, machine-written comments tend to: misquote or invent the rules and shortcuts they cite; stack headings on a short reply; sound like a clerk or support desk ("I carefully reviewed", "apologies for any inconvenience", "please point out specific issues and I will resolve them"); downplay their own origin, or say the words reflect the writer's own thoughts; ask critics what to fix; and dismiss doubts about origin as speculation, or ask critics to focus on content.

## Process and meta-communication tells

- Procedural change notes that stress reassurance: "ensured neutrality", "improved clarity and flow", "in compliance with the guidelines", "while preserving the original meaning", "added sourced content", "addressing reviewer feedback". Human notes name the specific change.
- Unusually exact naming of parameters, template names or markup in change notes.
- Pre-written "statements to the reviewer" that argue notability or compliance, or an AI-use disclosure that claims "full compliance".
- A canned profile page with "About me", "My interests", "Let's connect", emoji headings and bold labels.
- A sudden jump in an author's polish or voice, or a writing style that shifts in step with each model generation. The reverse supports human authorship: the same quirks across years, including before late 2022.
- Many fast, low-value edits across unrelated pages can use machine text, but fast adding of machine text alone is not evidence of bad faith.

## Differences between models

Each model and version has its own habits. Studies of 2024 and 2025 models found ChatGPT and Grok more prone to broader-context framing than Gemini and Claude, and Gemini and Claude replies more concise. What is typical of one generation does not hold for another.

## Content bias

Models mirror the skew of their training data, and weaker coverage of less-used languages and regions produces more invention. Treat claims about under-documented topics with extra care.

## Signs of human writing

- Text dated before 2022-11-30, the public launch of ChatGPT, cannot be from that kind of tool.
- A person can explain why they made a choice or mistake, can name the real source, and can say how a bad link arose. A model-pasted passage often cannot be defended.
- Humans use simple "is/has" phrases, plain verbs over stiff ones (wrote, moved, used, tried, died, not authored, relocated, utilized, attempted, passed away), superlatives and definitive claims, qualifiers and intensifiers (very, perhaps, tends to), and wordy phrases such as "as a result of", "in order to", "the fact that".

## Ineffective indicators

These are not reliable signs and sometimes point the other way. Wrongly accusing a writer of using AI harms trust, and confirmation bias is easy to fall into.

- Perfect grammar: skilled writers exist.
- A mix of casual and formal registers, or "clinical yet emotional" tone: common in people from technical fields, young writers, playful writers, neurodivergent writers, and multi-author documents.
- Bland or robotic prose: machine text skews positive and wordy and may not read as robotic to someone unfamiliar with it.
- Fancy, academic or formal prose in general: only specific words and patterns are overused, not elevated style as such.
- Transition words alone ("Additionally", "Consequently", "Notably"): older models overused a few, but human essays use them and many style guides accept them.
- Unsourced content: unsourced text is old and common, and current chatbots often add citations (which may be false).
- Strange wikitext or markup glitches, or correct markup: these usually come from editors and tools, not models.

## Historical indicators (2022 to 2025, much rarer now)

Useful for finding older, undetected machine text.

- Promotional, brochure or travel-guide tone (2023-2025): nestled, in the heart of, breathtaking, groundbreaking, renowned, boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, diverse array, natural beauty, commitment to, showcasing, exemplifies, featuring. It faded after 2024, and machine pieces still often are promotional in what they include (awards, coverage), if less in wording. Models avoided blunt superlatives like "the best".
- Knowledge-cutoff statements (2022-2023): "as of my last knowledge update", "up to my last training update".
- Section summaries: a "Conclusion" section, or a paragraph that restates its own point ("In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall").
- Prompt refusal: "as an AI language model", "I'm sorry", "I cannot offer medical advice, but..." plus an offer of an alternative.
- Abrupt cut-offs mid-sentence from a length limit. A bad copy-paste can do the same, and a copied source can look alike.
- Synonym cycling and repetition avoidance (see Style).

## Judgement notes

- A single "crucial" is not proof. Three tells in one paragraph is a signal to rewrite the paragraph from the facts.
- A hit in a quotation, a code block or a proper noun stays. The lint script cannot tell; the author decides.
- The lint script (`scripts/lint-prose.sh`) checks only mechanical tells: words, set phrases, placeholders and residue. It cannot check significance puffery, rhythm, structure or false sources.
- Do not treat every flagged pattern as an error. "Not only... but also" is correct when both halves are true and a reader might expect only one.
